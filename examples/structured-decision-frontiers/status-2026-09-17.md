# Local specialist and compound results — 2026-09-17

## What was tested

Three exact offerings ran the same 40 pinned BFCL v4 single-turn cases three
times on the 64 GB M5:

1. Granite 4 Micro 3B, MLX 8-bit, as a single tool-calling specialist.
2. Qwen3.8 27B, MLX oQ4e with reasoning enabled, as a single-model control.
3. Granite first, with Qwen selectively allowed to veto a proposed single-tool
   call by returning no call.

The panel includes eight cases from each of `simple_python`, `multiple`,
`parallel`, `parallel_multiple`, and `irrelevance`. It is not the full BFCL
leaderboard and contains only 40 distinct tasks.

## Result

| Offering | Success | p95 latency | Heavy calls/case | Stable case pattern |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | --- |
| Granite 4 Micro 8-bit | 114/120 (95.00%) | 1.415 s | 0 | 38 always pass, 2 always fail |
| Qwen3.8 27B oQ4e | 113/120 (94.17%) | 21.649 s | 1.00 | 37 always pass, 1 variable, 2 always fail |
| Granite + Qwen no-call veto | 116/120 (96.67%) | 11.937 s | 0.45 | 38 always pass, 1 variable, 1 always fails |

Granite and the compound system are residents of both published frontiers:

- outcome × p95 latency; and
- outcome × heavy-model calls per case.

Qwen alone is dominated by Granite on this workload: it is less accurate and
slower. That is evidence for using a small specialist here, not evidence that
Granite is a better general model.

The compound result reports the primary separately. Granite produced 114/120
successful primary answers inside the compound run. Qwen rescued 2 of its 6
errors and harmed none of its 114 successes. Both rescues were repetitions of
`irrelevance_68`; `irrelevance_239` remained wrong in every offering. The gain
is therefore real in this run but narrow and not statistically independent
across all 120 rows.

## Why the policy is constrained

A first policy simply replaced Granite with Qwen on triggered cases. Its
one-repeat smoke scored 37/40 (92.5%), invoked Qwen 18 times, and had roughly
12-second p95 latency. It damaged a correct parallel call and added no net
quality. That policy is not a frontier resident.

The successful policy instead uses Qwen as a one-way semantic veto:

- Granite remains responsible for tool names and arguments.
- Qwen runs only after Granite proposes a call while exactly one tool is
  available, or after a malformed/contract-invalid primary result.
- For the ordinary trigger, Qwen can change the answer only to “no call.”

This rule comes from the components' intended roles and visible runtime state.
It does not inspect benchmark labels or case IDs.

## Co-residency and memory

After both candidates were loaded, oMLX reported two resident models using
20,649,357,738 bytes against a 55,662,788,608-byte model-memory ceiling. The
health observation is retained in
[`granite-qwen-omlx-coresidency-2026-09-17.json`](granite-qwen-omlx-coresidency-2026-09-17.json).
The measurements therefore do not include llama-swap eviction/reload time.
Context and KV-cache growth still consume additional memory; this observation
is not a 256K-context capacity proof.

## What this establishes

- A specialist model can dominate a much larger general model on a narrow
  stage, so local recommendations must be workload-specific.
- At least one semantic two-model policy is nondominated against both measured
  components. Compound offerings therefore have evidence beyond a hypothetical
  category.
- Most combinations are still bad. The earlier GPT-OSS→Qwen router and the
  naive Granite→Qwen replacement are negative controls.
- Speculative target+draft systems are a different compound class: they seek
  speed while preserving target output, whereas this cascade deliberately may
  change the decision.
